# Day 8 Reflection

### What was the most difficult concept in the course, and how did you overcome it?
The most challenging concept in the course was designing systems that maintain strict data consistency under high concurrent load. Initially, I thought scaling simply meant adding more stateless web servers behind a load balancer. However, I learned that high concurrency shifts the real bottleneck to the database layer. In TicketHub, handling 200,000 users competing for 20,000 seats exposed the dangers of race conditions and double-booking. I overcame this by studying database isolation levels, atomic conditional updates (`UPDATE ... WHERE status = 'AVAILABLE'`), and temporary row-locking mechanisms. Diagramming the state transitions step by step helped me visualize how database transactions, idempotency keys, and constraints work together to guarantee correctness.

### Which part of your capstone would you improve, based on the feedback you received?
Based on the feedback received, I would improve the depth of my failure-mode analysis and edge-case handling. Specifically, I would refine how the architecture handles delayed payment webhooks and expired hold reconciliation. If a payment succeeds right as a 10-minute hold expires, the system needs an automated compensation mechanism (either auto-refunding the customer or reserving an equivalent alternative seat). Providing clearer sequence diagrams and concrete retry policies for background workers would make the design more resilient and production-ready.

### What will you learn next?
Next, I plan to dive deeper into practical distributed systems engineering. I want to gain hands-on experience implementing Redis caching patterns, setting up message brokers like RabbitMQ or Apache Kafka, and conducting simulated load tests using tools like k6 or Locust. Moving from architectural theory to benchmarking real code under stress will help me build systems that are fast, resilient, and secure.
